How to answer behavioural interview questions using STAR

By InterviewCrack · Updated 17 October 2026

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Use it to explain a real experience: set the context briefly, state your responsibility, describe your own actions, and finish with the outcome and what you learned.

What belongs in each part?

  • Situation: Give the context the listener needs. What problem or constraint mattered?
  • Task: State your responsibility. What were you expected to accomplish?
  • Action: Explain the decisions and steps you personally took. Make clear where colleagues contributed.
  • Result: Describe the actual outcome, what evidence you have, and what you would repeat or change.

Worked example: “Tell me about a time a plan changed”

Situation: “During a student project, the external data service became unavailable a week before our demonstration.”

Task: “I was responsible for the dashboard and needed to help the team deliver a usable demonstration.”

Action: “I confirmed the outage, explained the risk to the team, and proposed a clearly labelled sample dataset. I separated data loading from the view so we could restore the service later. A teammate prepared the sample data while I added an unavailable-service message.”

Result: “We demonstrated the main flow on time and explicitly told the audience which data was simulated. I learned to plan for external-service failures before the final week.”

This fictional example demonstrates structure. Build your answer from a genuine experience; do not adopt its events as your own.

How much detail should I include?

Keep context brief enough that your actions remain the centre of the answer. Practise a concise version, then prepare details for follow-up questions. If the interviewer asks about a trade-off, explain the alternatives you considered and why you chose one.

What if I do not have a numerical result?

Use an observable outcome you can honestly support: a completed handover, an agreed decision, a resolved problem, or a lesson applied later. Do not invent a percentage to make the answer sound stronger. If the outcome was disappointing, explain what you learned and changed.

A worksheet for your next answer

  1. Choose one prompt: a disagreement, a mistake, a deadline, or a new responsibility.
  2. Write one sentence for the situation and one for your task.
  3. List two or three actions with “I” where you personally acted.
  4. Write the outcome and one lesson.
  5. Say the answer aloud without reading. Check whether each action connects to the original problem.
